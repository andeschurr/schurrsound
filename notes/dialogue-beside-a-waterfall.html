Recording clean dialogue beside a waterfall

A big scene on Lord of the Flies, a few metres from a waterfall. Here’s how we got clean dialogue anyway, and the one thing that made post’s day.

On Lord of the Flies, the BBC and Netflix series we shot in Langkawi, there’s a big scene that plays out right beside a waterfall.

There’s no plug-in for that, that’s for sure. And there wasn’t one magic trick either. It was a handful of decisions, made in the right order, and behind all of them one simple idea: don’t fight the location. Work with it, and record it.

Most producers I’ve worked with will say at some point, “we’ll fix it in post.” Fair enough. For most problems that’s true, because post can do amazing things these days.

A roaring waterfall is one of the few times it isn’t, and it’s worth knowing why, because it shapes everything you do on the day.

Why you can’t fix a waterfall later

Most noise you can take out has a pattern. A generator hums on one note, air conditioning sits in one band, a fridge cycles on and off. Noise reduction is very good now at finding a pattern like that and lifting it out while leaving the voice alone.

Falling water has no pattern. Neither do cicadas, which is why a New Zealand bush location in summer can be every bit as tough as a waterfall in Malaysia. Both are a wall of random noise, sitting right on top of the part of the voice that makes the words clear. Try to pull the noise out and the consonants go with it, and the actor sounds like they’re talking through a wet pillow.

There’s always ADR, and it has its place. Sometimes it saves a word or two, and sometimes it gives the director something they didn’t have on the day. But it costs money and time, and it rarely has quite the same performance. Post can only work with what the actor gave you on the day.

So if there’s any chance of saving a line on location, we go for it, every time. Any experienced mixer feels that. But never at the cost of the director’s vision or the performance. Hurt either of those and it was all for nothing.

First: get the boom in close

The short version

Get the boom as close as the frame lets you, point it away from the falls, and keep the same angle all day.

Getting close is the big one. It’s the only free improvement you get on a location.

Halve the distance between the mic and the actor’s mouth and you gain about six decibels on the voice. The waterfall is all around you, so it stays exactly as loud as it was. That’s a big win for nothing.

Then there’s where you point it. Aim the mic away from the falls, not across them. It sounds obvious written down, but on a busy set it’s the first thing to slip.

On the coverage, the boom operator picks an angle and sticks with it: always from underneath, always from above, or always from the same side. Keeping the angle the same matters as much as keeping the distance the same. What really gives a water scene away is the noise jumping around. If the falls sit at one level in the wide and another in the close-up, you hear every cut.

On the wide, forget all that. There’s nowhere for the boom to go.

So it all comes down to talking with the camera department and the director. How tight is the frame? Could a slightly longer lens give us half a metre of boom room? Is there a shot order that gets us in close on the coverage, even if the wide is a write-off?

I’m not there to tell a director how to shoot. They bring the vision. Every role on set has its place, and mine is to speak up when there’s a problem, and when there isn’t, to get on with it.

Directors usually have a few ways a scene could go, and if I stay quiet the other options never come up. So I mention it, quickly, between setups, and they can take it or leave it. You can do a lot of good for the sound without costing the pictures or the story anything. The mixers I look up to all work this way: quick, easy to work with, and respectful of everything else in the film, which has every right to be there, often more than sound.

Adyt Santoso was on boom for this scene, and a lot of why it worked is down to him. He takes the boom right to the edge of the frame, take after take. He’s fearless, and that opens up what’s possible. It comes from trust, built over months on this show and years of working together before it.

Second: the radio mics, again and again

The short version

There’s no clever trick here. Keep checking, and when a rig stops working, go back in and sort it.

Working with a young cast in a rainforest, with their chaperones, across lots of locations out in the bush, is a job of its own.

On Lord of the Flies the costumes fall apart as the series goes on, on purpose, which is exactly right for the story and exactly what makes hiding a mic hard. The shirts are the first to go. Each week there was less fabric to work with, and more heat, sweat and running about. Little air pockets in the boys’ belts gave the mics somewhere to hide, and now and then the boys turned up in clothes they’d salvaged, which gave us somewhere new.

Toto and Muel Yow in costume were brilliant the whole way through. The rigs only worked because of them.

The DPA mics did their job, rigged and re-rigged at every costume change. You move it, re-tape it, check it again, and accept that a rig that worked yesterday might not work today.

Reza Feryanto was our sound utility on that unit. Reza never gives up, and he always has one more trick. Every time clothing noise, wind or movement caught us out, he’d go back in and try the next thing.

That’s really the point, and it took me a while to see it. Paying attention isn’t enough on its own. You have to be willing to keep going back.

Next to a waterfall the lav works a bit differently, and the comparison with the boom is interesting. It isn’t always cleaner. Each mic gives you a different balance of noise and voice. A lav under costume is shielded from a lot of what’s coming from behind and below, and the boom gets no help with that at all. The boom, up close, gets the clearest voice. In the mix, having both is worth far more than having either one perfect.

Third: record the place on its own

The short version

Whenever you get the chance, record the location on its own, and do it properly. Post will love you for it.

This is the part I’d do again on every location like this one.

We recorded the falls and the rainforest around them in full surround, on a Sennheiser AMBEO VR mic, the one with four capsules. We didn’t stop at thirty seconds. We recorded hours of it, from lots of positions and distances, at different times of day, in long takes, some with the unit there and some with the place to ourselves. A lot of it we got at weekends and before sunrise, when nothing else was moving.

Nobody could take the water out of my dialogue track, and that was never the idea. With the location on its own tracks, the dialogue only has to carry the words. Post can lift the lines where they matter and let the rainforest we recorded fill in everything around them.

A lot of the waterfall you hear in the finished show is the one we recorded, at the times of day we chose, placed where the story needed it. The post team sourced the rest themselves, to separate out the parts they needed.

Post was led by supervising sound editor and re-recording mixer Niv Adiri, who won both the Academy Award and the BAFTA for sound on Gravity. All that material only mattered because his team knew what to do with it.

Which is why I’d happily never hear “fix it in post” again. It makes it sound like post is there to rescue something that went badly wrong, or to invent something that was never there, and that’s more a visual effects conversation than a sound one. What post really does is polish. It takes what you give it and stretches it as far as it’ll go, which is a very long way in the right hands. So give them something worth stretching.

What it comes down to

There wasn’t one fix. It was a lot of small things: not wasting anyone’s time, being ready on cue, working hand in hand with costume and makeup, being right there for every block, and speaking up only when there was a problem. And the big one: recording the location properly whenever we had the chance.

Most location sound problems get solved with a quick chat on set.

The noisy places are almost always there for a reason. Either there’s no way around them, or they look wonderful, and usually both. So embrace it, work with it, talk about it, and get the best out of it.

Further

Marc Munden and Jack Thorne talk about how they approached the adaptation, including why the boys find what they find at the waterfall, in The Vision Behind Lord of the Flies. It’s a good watch, and you can see how carefully the two of them thought it all through.

Coming next: recording the rehearsals. I record every one, to the amusement of other mixers. You get the performances before anyone gets wet, and post can take words from them, so you hardly ever need wild lines.
