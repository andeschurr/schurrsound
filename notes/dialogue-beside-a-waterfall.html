Recording clean dialogue beside a waterfall

On Lord of the Flies we had a full scene played metres from a waterfall. There is no plug-in for that. There was no single trick either. There was a stack of decisions, made in a particular order, and underneath all of them one idea: do not fight the environment, record it.

Every producer I have worked with has, at some point, said a version of the same sentence: we will fix it in post. For most problems that is fair enough. Post is extraordinary now. But a waterfall is one of the specific cases where it is not true, and it is worth understanding why, because the reason dictates everything you do on the day.

Why a waterfall cannot be fixed later

Most noise you can remove has a shape. A generator hums at a frequency. Air conditioning sits in a band. A fridge cycles. Modern noise reduction is very good at finding something with a shape and subtracting it while leaving the voice intact.

Falling water has no shape. Neither do cicadas, which is why a New Zealand summer bush location can be just as unforgiving as a Malaysian waterfall. Both are broadband and effectively random, and both sit directly on top of the frequency range where speech intelligibility lives. The noise is not next to the dialogue. It is inside it. Try to subtract it and you take the consonants and sibilants with it, and the actor ends up sounding like they are talking through a pillow, underwater.

There is always ADR. Sometimes a line genuinely cannot be saved and the honest call is to flag it, move on, and let the actor re-voice it in a studio months later. But ADR costs money, costs schedule, and almost never carries the same performance. Post can only optimise what the actor actually gave on the day. So when there is a chance a line can be salvaged in the field, we run with that, every time.

The noise is not the enemy

Here is the part that took me years to understand properly. The waterfall is not something to be defeated. I embrace the sound of whatever environment I am standing in. That is true of everything I do, and it is one of the reasons Marc Munden wanted me to mic the jungle.

The rainforest in Langkawi has a depth, richness and variety that no library can match. We were never going to make it quiet, and we did not want it quiet. So why hand post the enormous job of inventing that world from scratch, when I can give them what it actually sounds like, recorded with microphones in the right positions, so they can raise it, lower it, fade it in and out as the story needs?

To be clear about scale: this is not a hundred microphones hanging in trees. At any one time I had one or two out for environment. The dialogue is still everything, and dialogue is a mono microphone on an actor. There is no surround version of a performance.

So the job on the day is not to eliminate the waterfall. It is to record a dialogue track where the voice sits far enough above the water that the mix has somewhere to go, and to hand post a recorded environment they can shape on their own terms rather than fight against mine.

Decision one: the boom, as tight as the frame allows

Nothing beats proximity. The inverse square law is the only genuinely free improvement available on a location, and it is worth more than any piece of equipment in the bag. Halving the distance between the capsule and the actor's mouth gains you roughly six decibels on the voice, while the waterfall, which is effectively an infinitely large source spread across the whole environment, barely moves at all.

The other half of it is aim. Point the microphone away from the falls, not across them. Obvious when written down, and yet the pressure of a moving set makes it the first thing to slip. On coverage you commit to an angle and hold it: always from underneath, or always from above, or always from the same side. Consistency of angle matters as much as consistency of distance.

Where this stops being available is the wide. If the frame is genuinely wide there is no boom position, and the radio mics carry the scene alone. That is not a failure, it is arithmetic. It does mean the coverage has to earn back what the wide gives away.

Which means the whole thing is a conversation with the camera department and the director, not a problem to solve with equipment. How tight is the frame. Could a slightly longer lens buy fifty centimetres of boom room. Is there a shot order that lets us get in close on the coverage even if the wide is a write-off.

It is worth saying how that conversation should go. It is not my job to tell a director how to shoot a scene. The vision is what they bring, and the narrative, the performance and the visuals matter more than my track does. But directors usually have several ways a scene could go, not one, and if I stay quiet the sound option never enters the room. So you speak up, quickly, on the floor, between setups, and you offer rather than demand. Every senior mixer I admire works this way: collaborative, fast, and with real tolerance for everything else that has to be right for the film to work.

On this scene Adyt Santoso was on boom, and a great deal of what made it work was simply him being braver about the frame line than a less experienced operator would have been. Adyt is fearless in a way that changes what is possible. That is not a technical decision. That is trust, built over four months on this show and many years of working together before it.

For producers

If a scene sits beside water, wind or traffic, the most useful thing you can give the sound department is not equipment budget. It is thirty seconds of the director's ear, on the floor, between setups, and a shot order that gets the tight coverage done before the light goes.

Ask me on the floor, not in an email. I answer faster and I am more useful.

Decision two: radio mics, rigged and re-rigged

Concealing radio microphones on a young cast in a rainforest is its own discipline. On Lord of the Flies the costumes degrade progressively across the series, which is exactly right for the story and exactly wrong for concealment. Each week there was less fabric available to work with, and more heat, sweat and movement to contend with. Occasionally the boys are found wearing salvaged clothing, which quietly gave us somewhere to hide a capsule again.

DPA capsules did the work, rigged and re-rigged as the costumes changed. There is no clever answer here, only attention. You reposition, you re-tape, you re-check after every costume note, and you accept that a rig which worked yesterday will not work today.

Marc Munden and Jack Thorne talked about their approach to the adaptation, including why the boys find what they find at the waterfall, in this interview. Worth watching if you want the thinking behind the scene.

Utilities on that unit was Reza Feryanto, and the honest truth is that lav quality on a shoot like this is decided almost entirely by how many times somebody was willing to go back in and redo a rig that was nearly good enough. Reza is exactly that person. He never gives up, and he always has another trick left.

Beside a waterfall the lav earns its keep differently from usual. It is not necessarily cleaner than the boom, because it is picking up the same broadband wash. What it gives you is a second perspective with a different ratio, and a fallback for the moment the boom has to pull out of a wide. There is a physical reason it helps too: the capsule sits against the body, and the body absorbs and shades a surprising amount of what is arriving from behind and below. In the mix, having both perspectives is worth far more than having either one be perfect.

Decision three: perspective consistency across takes

The thing that quietly ruins water scenes is not the noise level, it is the noise changing. If the wide take has the falls at one level and the close-up has them at another, every cut in the sequence draws attention to itself, and post ends up doing a great deal of unglamorous work smoothing a floor that should have been consistent to begin with.

So you hold your positions. You note where the boom was. You resist the temptation to creep closer on take four because it sounds better in your headphones, if creeping closer means the take will not cut against the others.

With one honest caveat: consistency only beats quality once the track is usable to begin with. On a scene like this one I went as close as I possibly could, and then held that. Get above the water first. Be consistent second.

Decision four: the one that made post's day

The part I would do again on every location like this: a proper library of surround wild tracks, captured on location in pristine resolution.

We recorded the falls and the surrounding rainforest as full surround material on a Sennheiser AMBEO VR microphone. Not a token thirty seconds. A real library: multiple positions, multiple distances, different times of day, long unbroken takes, recorded both with the unit present and with the location entirely to itself. A good deal of it was captured at weekends, when nothing else was moving.

That changes what post is being asked to do. Instead of trying to pull my waterfall out from underneath the dialogue, they now have the waterfall on its own separate track. They can turn it down where the words matter and bring it back up between them. The waterfall you hear in the finished show is not the one that was fighting my dialogue track. It is the one we recorded on purpose, placed where the story wants it.

Post on the show was led by supervising sound editor and re-recording mixer Niv Adiri.

The waterfall is not the obstacle. It is part of the environment the scene lives in, and the job is to record it so it can work with the performance instead of against it.

In short

There was no single fix. There was proximity, held consistently. There were radio mics re-rigged more times than anyone counted. There was a boom operator willing to live on the frame line. And there was a decision, made early, to record the environment properly rather than try to erase it later.

The one thing I would ask of a production is not budget. It is the conversation. Flagging a location as noisy is not the same as asking to move: a waterfall usually needs to be in the shot, and a power station behind a tree usually does not, and the difference between those two situations is worth thirty seconds of discussion rather than a memo. Get that conversation happening early and cheaply, and almost everything else sorts itself out on the floor.
