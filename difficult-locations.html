Open sea

DramaLord of the FliesBBC and Netflix, 2026

Almost four months across the islands off Langkawi. Days began before dawn with a half-hour boat ride, then an hour on foot through jungle, the kit on our backs in waterproof bags.

At the shoreline the surf is too loud to rely on the boom alone, and a boom magnifies whatever is around it. So the dialogue also came from DPA microphones hidden where there was nowhere to hide them: air pockets built into the boys’ leather belts, worked out with the costume department.

Tides came in and weather rolled through, so the three of us split three ways: one on set, one with costume and makeup, one guarding the rig.

DocumentaryOn an Unknown BeachFeature documentary, 2016

A film by Adam Luxton and Summer Agnew. Three weeks at sea on NIWA’s research vessel Tangaroa, over the Chatham Rise east of New Zealand, following deep-sea coral scientist Di Tracey as the ship’s cameras went a kilometre down to check the state of the coral and how trawl fishing had affected it.

I was the production sound mixer. After a day or two the ship’s motion settles into a comforting rhythm, and you sleep no matter how the ship is tossed, snug in your bunk. I went on every deck and into every part of the ship and made a 3D map of the whole vessel in sound, so post-production could create something unique with the soundscape.

Jungle and waterfalls

DramaLord of the FliesBBC and Netflix, 2026

Humidity at 85 per cent, and rain as the great leveller. We moved every fifteen to thirty minutes to a new patch of bush, so dense there was barely room to squeeze through with the kit around my waist.

A major scene played out metres from a waterfall, and there’s no plug-in for that, that’s for sure. Every speaking actor had a DPA 6061 with its splash kit, and I spoke up early for the coverage the edit would need.

One of my secrets

Something I do, to other mixers’ amusement, is record every rehearsal, so the performances are logged before anyone gets wet. It saves a great deal of time: very few wild lines are ever needed on location, because post can find the words they need in my rehearsals. And like ADR, wild lines can sound a bit forced, so we try to avoid them.

DramaLord of the FliesBBC and Netflix, 2026

DocumentaryGet Out Alive with Bear GryllsNBC, 2013

Ten teams of two in the mountains around Wānaka, in New Zealand’s South Island, with a crew of 160. I was the production sound mixer on one of the camera teams, alongside cinematographer Simon Temple, who brought me onto the job. These are the Southern Alps, the mountains where Sir Edmund Hillary learned to climb before he and Tenzing Norgay reached the top of Everest in 1953.

Through the month of March we sprinted up mountains of slate, dodging the spiky Spaniard grass, and went through waist-deep streams, glaciers, and rainforest that took 300 mm of rain in two days. Often we were choppered to high positions, to be in place before the contestants came through. The crew soon got used to the mountain goat life.

Driving and chases

DramaHunt for the Wilderpeople and Monkey Man

The chase sequences on Hunt for the Wilderpeople taught me to hold the point of focus while everything around it moves. The fight choreography on Monkey Man, shot on a gimbal through 360 degrees, taught me to trust my radio mics.

A Wisycom MTP61 transmitter into a Betso Sharkie antenna on my bag rig lets me stand well back, or ride in the follow vehicle, and still hear every word. The transmitter records on board as well, so every line is recorded twice.

For riders the microphone goes inside the helmet, out of the wind. Engines and passes are recorded on their own, so the edit can balance the dialogue against the machine.

Crew from the region

My own team is from Southeast Asia: boom operator Adyt Santoso and sound assistant Reza Feryanto are both Indonesian. I also crew locally. My network of boom operators and assistants runs deep across Malaysia, and grows every year across the region, from Bangkok to Vietnam. I work throughout Southeast Asia: Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, the Philippines and Cambodia.

When a US or UK production flies in, it often wants a head of sound who works the way it does, someone it can rely on from day one. I can be that person, with the region’s best boom operators and assistants alongside me.

On Lord of the Flies we worked very closely with the on-set team and made sure we were never a problem for them. On the day, our microphones went into the sets just before their final dressing. We help them, they help us, because it’s invigorating to be part of a team making something extraordinary. It showed: the series’ sound was nominated for an Emmy.
