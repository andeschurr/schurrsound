BBC / Netflix, 2026  ·  Director Marc Munden Drama

Lord of the Flies

Production sound mixer and head of department, Langkawi, Malaysia. Emmy nomination, Outstanding Sound Mixing.

  • Almost four months across several islands, in jungle and ocean.
  • A department of three: me, boom operator Adyt Santoso and sound assistant Reza Feryanto.
  • Around thirty young actors, about half of them on radio mics.
  • Humidity at 85 per cent, and surf too loud to rely on the boom alone.
  • Nominated for the 2026 Emmy for Outstanding Sound Mixing.

The brief

Normally you record the dialogue and keep everything else out. Marc Munden wanted the opposite: capture the scene as it is performed and, in that same take, whatever the island gives you. A thunderclap rolling in behind a line. A bird call landing at the right moment. He wants the trees, he wants the ocean. Needless to say, I had microphones all over the place. Well, in actual fact, only one or two.

Microphones with nowhere to hide

The boys are stranded on a tropical island, and their costume falls apart on purpose, episode by episode. Imagine any kids running loose on a deserted island: the shirts are the first thing to come off. So with the costume department we built air pockets into the boys’ leather belts for the lapel mics to hide in. That gave us a perfect distance between mic and voice, because the performances are loud. They’re on an island, shouting much of the time. When a scene goes quiet, we adjust: the boom comes in, the shot gets tighter, and it becomes a more intimate scene.

Into the water

The boys lay on the beach, crouched and walked through the waves, so the microphone positions changed from scene to scene.

The waterfall

A major scene played out metres from a waterfall, and there’s no plug-in for that, that’s for sure. It means being on the ball the whole time: what the waterfall is doing to the sound, which shots are coming, what the director and the DOP need, and making sure what I need is worked into the schedule. The island on set

I went out before dawn to record the bird chorus, and when I found something special I sent it to Marc. For the scene where Simon approaches the boar's head on its stick, he played one of those bird calls live, just before the take. The bird the boys heard is the bird the audience hears.

Two nights on the islands

I left ambisonic microphones out overnight, close to the water. The nights here sound like nowhere else: the azan, the call to prayer, carried across the strait from the mainland, with the waves, the night insects and the drip of water into the mangroves. Worlds like this are here for any production that needs them.

Piggy and the conch

Piggy finally manages to blow the conch, and at the very end you hear his breath inside the shell and the faint ocean in it. It looks like a happy accident. It is also what a careful rig is set up to catch.

Recorded live

The chants, the arguments and the panic were captured on location, with those kids, in that place. You can hear that it’s real.

Four months

Almost four months of islands, jungle, ocean and weather, with a young cast. Our first assistant director ran the shoot like an ultra-marathon, and so did we: warm-ups every morning, the gear well looked after, plenty of sunscreen, and everyone looking out for each other.
